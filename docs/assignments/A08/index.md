# A8 – Gears

## Objective
This week we were tasked to make a gear box design with the given gear ratio and shaft diameters. We needed to test weather the given shaft diameters were sufficient for the given power in the design as well as come up with our own journal bearings and housing for the gears. 

## Analyze
One of first things I had to do was test to see if the given diameters for the shafts were sufficient enough to withstand the power that was being generated by the gears.  I started by listing out my known values, which included the output torque of 1.25 in·lbf, output speed of 1100 rpm, and the gear ratio of 24:9. Since the gearbox is assumed to be ideal, I used the gear ratio to calculate an input torque of 0.46875 in·lbf and an input speed of 2933.33 rpm.

After finding these values, I created a free-body diagram showing the input torque and the opposing torque from the gear, along with the shaft's original diameter and length. I then used the power-transmission equation from my MEGR 2156 lecture, which referenced Machinery's Handbook. Before finding the minimum diameter, I calculated the transmitted power to be approximately 0.02182 HP. Using the power-transmission equation, I calculated a minimum required shaft diameter of approximately 0.0841 inches. Since the original diameter of 0.125 inches was larger than my calculated minimum, I determined that the input shaft was sufficient for the required power transmission under the assumed design conditions. ## Decide

After the analysis that the given diameters of the shafts were sufficient enough to hold the power of the gears I was able to decide to stick to the given diameters for my design. After that I was able to move on to making decisions about my journal bearings. Before I started my calculations for this section, I went to the resources listed in the assignment to choose my gears. I knew I needed to maintain the required 24:9 gear ratio, but I also wanted to make sure the gears would work with my given shaft diameters. This led me to choose an 18-tooth input gear and a 48-tooth output gear from the McMaster-Carr catalog. Both gears maintain the required ratio and have matching diametral pitches and pressure angles, allowing them to work together for my gearbox design. Since I already selected my 18-tooth and 48-tooth gears, I used their pitch diameters and the 20-degree pressure angle to calculate the tangential and radial forces. I started by using the torque and pitch diameter to calculate a tangential force of 2.50 lbf. From there, I used the pressure angle to find a radial force of approximately 0.910 lbf.

After finding both forces, I combined them to calculate a resultant gear force of approximately 2.66 lbf. For my initial design, I decided to use two journal bearings on each shaft with the gear positioned in the middle. Since the setup is symmetrical, I assumed the bearings would split the load evenly, giving me approximately 1.33 lbf per bearing. These calculations helped me figure out the loads my bearings need to support so I can move forward with choosing their dimensions and materials. Below is my work for this process. 

For the next part , I needed to calculate the pressure, velocity, and PV values for my journal bearings so I could figure out what materials would work best. I decided to start with a bearing length of 0.250 inches for both shafts and used the bearing loads I calculated in the previous section.

I started with the input shaft and calculated a bearing pressure of 42.56 psi, a surface velocity of approximately 96.00 ft/min, and a PV value of about 4,086 psi·ft/min. I then repeated the same calculations for the output shaft and got a pressure of 21.28 psi, a velocity of approximately 72.00 ft/min, and a PV value of about 1,532 psi·ft/min.

After comparing my results, I noticed that the input bearing had higher pressure and velocity values, mainly because the input shaft is smaller and rotates faster. These results gave me a better idea of what my bearings need to handle and will help me choose a material that stays within the allowable pressure, velocity, and PV limits. Next for my gearbox, I decided to go with SAE 841 oil-embedded bronze sleeve bearings from McMaster-Carr because they're pretty durable, help reduce friction, and don't need much extra lubrication. For my 0.125-inch input shaft, I chose bearing 6391K111, and for my 0.250-inch output shaft, I went with 6391K126. Both bearings have a length of 0.250 inches.

The bronze bearings have a max pressure of 2,000 psi, velocity of 1,200 ft/min, and PV of 50,000 psi·ft/min. From my Part 2b calculations, my highest values were 42.56 psi, 96 ft/min, and 4,086 psi·ft/min. Since my values are way below these limits, the material should work well for my design.
